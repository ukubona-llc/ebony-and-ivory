01

Two ground states

Beato teaches diatonic harmony from a YouTube lesson on I–IV–V blues changes: the triad (1–3–5) is the atomic unit, and it is structurally complete on its own. A seventh, when it shows up, is an addition to something already finished. The Gospel University never starts there. Its course begins with the seventh chord as the minimum viable object — tensions (9, 11, 13), extensions, and alterations (♭, ♭♭, ♯) come after, stacked on a tetradic floor, not a triadic one.

What "major" was hiding

In a triadic system, G and G7 both collapse to the same label — major. The distinction that actually predicts harmonic behavior is deferred. In a tetradic system they're different species from the start.

03

Rootless V7♭9 — four equal exits

Play a V7(♭9) and drop the root. What's left of G7(♭9) — B, D, F, A♭ — is a fully diminished seventh: four notes stacked in minor thirds, perfectly symmetrical, no privileged center. Any of the four can act as leading tone into a different key. That symmetry is the phenomenological core of holding tension without collapsing it.

04

Where the metaphor breaks

Gemini's line was elegant but ran the causality backwards.

Claude — grounded calibration

Softmax doesn't create the symmetry of the diminished chord — it normalizes preferences that already exist upstream. The pitch-class symmetry lives in the representation; softmax only governs the final commitment. A closer analogy sits one layer earlier:

pitch-class geometry→latent representation→context→attention→decision

The rootless dim7 resembles self-attention before the output projection more than it resembles softmax: every note relates equally to every other note, with no privileged center — the center only emerges once a larger musical context arrives. Softmax is the moment context has already broken the symmetry, not the thing that produces it.
